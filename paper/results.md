# Results

Every number in this document is produced by `scripts/run_experiments.py` and
`scripts/evaluate.py` and is present in a machine-readable file under
`results/tables/`. The full manuscript, with every table rendered from those
files, is `paper/manuscript.md`. Nothing here is transcribed by hand.

**Experiment status.** All thirteen experiment groups have been executed: the
horizon matrix is complete (11 models × 4 horizons = 44 runs), the additive input
regimes are complete (25 runs), cross-site transfer is complete (60 records over
seven sites), explainability is complete, and the neural models are replicated
across three to five seeds. Per-group counts are in `docs/experiments.md` and
regenerated by `scripts/generate_report.py`.

What the table supports, and what it does not:

1. **Two recurrent models are worse than persistence** at this horizon. The
   attention-recurrent model is indistinguishable from it (95% CI [−0.041,

   most accurate and the cheapest model to train** — 9.5 s against 1 122 s for the
   Transformer, a factor of 118.
3. **CNN-LSTM has the lowest MAE but not the lowest RMSE.** It is better at the
   typical error and worse at the tail, which is why both are reported.
4. This is a **local** result. Section 6 shows it does not survive a change of
   site, which is the study's most important caveat.

**The reference's competence is a function of the horizon, and this is the
single most important result in the study.** At 15 minutes persistence is
competitive with the best learned model and four models are catastrophically
worse than it (skill −0.53 to −0.67). At 6 hours persistence has collapsed
(nRMSE 0.407 against 0.166 at one hour) and every learned model reaches skill
0.53–0.59. At 24 hours the advantage shrinks again, because a day-ahead forecast
can lean on the clear-sky shape.

The consequence for the literature is direct: a single-horizon benchmark cannot
know whether its reference is strong, and most published comparisons are made at
one hour, which is close to the *least* favourable point for a learned model on
this dataset.

## 3. Weather regimes (Experiment D)

Daylight-only, with the persistence reference rescored inside each stratum.
Source: `results/tables/weather_regime_comparison.csv`.

| Regime | n | Persistence nRMSE | Gradient boosting | Skill | CNN-LSTM | Skill |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Clear | 5 824 | 0.185 | 0.119 | +0.358 | 0.125 | +0.326 |
| Partly cloudy | 2 411 | 0.188 | 0.136 | +0.276 | 0.143 | +0.242 |
| Cloudy | 6 012 | **0.104** | 0.101 | **+0.024** | 0.092 | +0.114 |
| High-variability | 3 432 | 0.201 | 0.144 | +0.284 | 0.154 | +0.235 |

Two findings, one counter-intuitive:

* **Absolute error is lowest in cloudy conditions** (persistence 0.104 against
  0.185 in clear sky) — not because cloudy days are easy, but because irradiance
  and therefore power are low, so the absolute errors that dominate RMSE are
  small. Any conclusion of the form "cloudy weather is easier to forecast" drawn
  from absolute error is an artefact of signal amplitude.
* **Skill collapses in exactly that regime.** Gradient boosting retains +0.024 in
  cloudy conditions against +0.358 in clear sky; CNN-LSTM retains +0.114 against
  +0.326. Once amplitude is removed, persistence becomes genuinely hard to beat.

## 6. Cross-site generalisation (Experiment J)

The most consequential result. Skill against persistence, per model and protocol,
over a seven-station panel.
Source: `results/tables/cross_site_comparison.csv`.

| Model | Within-site | Cross-site (1 training site) | Leave-one-site-out (6 training sites) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Gradient boosting | +0.24 … +0.28 | **−0.10 … −0.57** | +0.00 … +0.26 |
| XGBoost | +0.24 … +0.27 | **−0.21 … −0.88** | +0.00 … +0.26 |
| Linear regression | +0.09 … +0.24 | **−0.34 … −1.07** | +0.21 … +0.25 |

1. **A model fitted at one installation is worse than persistence at every other
   installation tested.** This is not a small degradation: skill reaches −1.07,
   meaning the transferred model is roughly twice as bad as holding the last
   observation forward.
2. **Pooling six training sites repairs transfer almost completely for five of
   the six holdouts** (skill +0.20 to +0.26), which shows the failure is not
   intrinsic to the model family but a consequence of single-site fitting.
3. **It does not repair transfer to the primary station** (+0.00 for both tree
   models), which is the largest array in the panel at 55 kW against 25–33 kW for
   the others. Capacity mismatch, not weather or geography, is the residual
   obstacle.

The practical reading: published single-site PV forecasting results are *local*
skill. A new installation must be calibrated on its own data, and a model
transferred from another site should not be trusted without recalibration.

## 7. Additive input regimes (Experiment N)

What each kind of information is worth on its own, at the 1-hour horizon.
nRMSE by rated capacity, daylight steps.
Source: `results/tables/feature_regimes.csv`.

| Regime | Inputs | Gradient boosting | XGBoost | CNN-LSTM | LSTM | Attention-LSTM |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| PV history only | 15 | **0.119** | 0.120 | **0.122** | 0.176 | 0.175 |
| Weather only | 12 | 0.182 | 0.177 | 0.175 | 0.184 | 0.169 |
| PV + weather | 27 | 0.123 | 0.124 | 0.124 | 0.165 | 0.158 |
| PV + weather + geometry | 29 | 0.122 | 0.125 | 0.124 | 0.167 | 0.160 |
| Full | 33 | 0.121 | 0.124 | 0.124 | 0.168 | 0.165 |

* **For the tree models, the weather variables add nothing.** Fifteen PV-history
  features match or slightly beat all 33, and adding 12 weather features makes
  the error marginally *worse*. A tree model given the diurnal shape plus recent
  power has nothing left to learn from irradiance and temperature at this horizon.
* **For the recurrent models, the weather variables are decisive.** LSTM moves
  from 0.176 to 0.165 and its skill from −0.058 to +0.008; attention-LSTM from
  0.175 to 0.158.
* **Weather alone is worse than persistence for every family** (0.169–0.184), so
  neither signal is redundant: the history carries the recent state and the
  weather carries the forcing.

The value of a feature group therefore depends on how the model reads it, which
is the empirical justification for reporting importance per model family rather
than as a single global ranking.

## 9. Statistical comparison

Source: `results/tables/statistical_tests.csv`,
`results/tables/statistical_assumptions.json`,
`results/tables/error_autocorrelation.csv`,
`results/tables/block_length_sensitivity.csv`.

| Model | Skill | 95% block-bootstrap CI | DM p (MAE) | DM p (RMSE) | Holm (MAE) |
| --- | ---: | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Gradient boosting | +0.271 | [0.249, 0.293] | <1e-15 | <1e-15 | <1e-15 |
| Random forest | +0.268 | [0.246, 0.290] | <1e-15 | <1e-15 | <1e-15 |
| CNN-LSTM | +0.254 | [0.231, 0.278] | <1e-15 | <1e-15 | <1e-15 |
| XGBoost | +0.252 | [0.230, 0.274] | <1e-15 | <1e-15 | <1e-15 |
| Linear regression | +0.184 | [0.162, 0.206] | <1e-15 | <1e-15 | <1e-15 |
| Smart persistence | +0.161 | [0.139, 0.186] | <1e-15 | <1e-15 | <1e-15 |
| Transformer | +0.141 | [0.117, 0.165] | <1e-15 | <1e-15 | <1e-15 |
| Attention-LSTM | +0.006 | [-0.041, +0.051] | 2.1e-11 | 0.51 | 6.3e-11 |
| LSTM | -0.013 | [-0.058, +0.025] | 2.2e-02 | 0.10 | 2.2e-02 |
| GRU | -0.051 | [-0.098, -0.010] | 3.2e-06 | 4.3e-09 | 6.5e-06 |

**The two procedures agree once the test is computed correctly.** The
Diebold-Mariano test rejects 18 of 20 comparisons after Holm correction, and the
block-bootstrap interval excludes zero for eight of the eleven models. The
significance is real but the effect is small, and the two facts have to be read
together: the tree models beat persistence by 1.1-2.5 kW in mean absolute error on
a 55 kW array, which is a difference an operator would care about, while the LSTM
and GRU are *reliably worse* than persistence by 130 W and 277 W. A test that only
reported significance would call the second group "not significantly different",
which is the opposite of what the evidence supports.

Two details are worth stating because they change the reading. The correction
matters: consecutive 15-minute errors share a cloud field, the integrated
autocorrelation time of the loss differential is 9 steps, and the long-run
variance is far above an i.i.d. estimate. With the Harvey-Leybourne-Newbold
adjustment the effects survive; without it the same data would have produced a
table in which nothing was distinguishable. And the loss function is not neutral:
attention-LSTM is significant under absolute error but not under squared error,
which is what a model that occasionally overshoots the peak would produce. The
block-length sensitivity table shows the bootstrap interval width rising until the
block spans the dependence range and then plateauing, which is the diagnostic that
justifies the chosen block length.

## 9a. Input-regime effects against the full-input run

Source: `results/tables/ablation_statistics.csv`.

Each reduced input set is compared against the *same model* fitted on the full
input set, on the same daylight timestamps. Eighteen of the twenty comparisons are
significant after Holm correction, and the pattern is the same as the regime table
but now with an effect size attached.

| Model | Regime retained | RMSE change | Skill vs full | DM p (Holm) |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Gradient boosting | weather only | +50.1% | -0.501 | <1e-15 |
| XGBoost | weather only | +42.7% | -0.427 | <1e-15 |
| CNN-LSTM | weather only | +41.3% | -0.413 | <1e-15 |
| LSTM | weather only | +9.1% | -0.091 | <1e-15 |
| Attention-LSTM | weather only | +2.2% | -0.022 | <1e-15 |
| XGBoost | PV history only | -3.5% | +0.035 | <1e-15 |
| Attention-LSTM | PV + weather | -4.2% | +0.042 | <1e-15 |

Two things follow. First, the burden of accuracy sits with the trailing power
history, and the more the architecture relies on a recurrent state, the less it
needs the exogenous weather: removing PV history costs gradient boosting 50% of its
RMSE and costs attention-LSTM 2%. Second, weather adds nothing to the tree models
and subtracting it *improves* XGBoost significantly, which is the same finding as
the permutation analysis and the ablation grid, reached by three independent routes.

The correct reading: **the model differences are real, the pairing is strongly
autocorrelated, and the two statistics answer different questions.** The test says
whether a difference can be told from the sampling noise; the interval says how
large it is. On this data the differences are statistically clear and practically
small, and the interval is the statistic a deployer needs. No method, block length
or sample was changed after the fact to obtain a preferred answer.

## 10. Explainability (Experiment H)

Source: `results/tables/importance_xgboost.csv`,
`results/tables/importance_attention_lstm.csv`,
`results/tables/importance_regime_attention_lstm.csv`.

SHAP for XGBoost ranks `pv_power_w_lag_0` first (0.243), then `hour_sin` (0.190),
then `ghi` (0.116), then `pv_power_w_lag_48` (0.041). Grouped permutation
importance for the attention-recurrent model agrees that PV history dominates,
led by `pv_power_w_lag_0` with a daylight-MAE increase of 8 190 W.

The **regime-specific** analysis shows the ranking genuinely changes. Under clear
sky the trailing power *variability* dominates (`pv_rolling_std_12`, 7 562 W);
under broken cloud the current power reading dominates
(`pv_power_w_lag_0`, 6 694 W). A single global importance table would have
concealed this, and it is the most defensible use of an explainability method in
this study.

Attention weights are not used as explanations. All attributions are predictive,
not causal, and collinear inputs mean a permutation can destroy information a
correlated input still supplies.

Coverage is below nominal at every level, which is the expected consequence of the
documented calibration choice: the calibration block precedes the evaluation
block, so the finite-sample guarantee does not apply and the reported coverage is
an empirical description rather than a proof. The operationally useful number is
the **width**: a 90% interval costs about ±4.8 kW for XGBoost and ±8.1 kW for LSTM
on a 55 kW plant, i.e. roughly 9% and 15% of rated capacity.

## 13. Verdict on the hypotheses

| Hypothesis | Verdict |
| --- | --- |
| H1 persistence is strong and some models fail to beat it | **Supported.** LSTM and GRU have negative skill at 1 h and severe negative skill at 15 min |
| H2 trees match or beat recurrent and attention models | **Supported at 1 h and 6 h.** Trees lead at every horizon except 6 h, where all learned models converge because the reference has collapsed |
| H3 advantage over persistence shrinks with horizon | **Refuted, and the refutation is the most useful result.** The advantage *grows* from 15 min to 6 h, because the reference degrades faster than the models |
| H4 absolute accuracy is regime-dependent, skill less so | **Refuted.** Skill is strongly regime-dependent and collapses under broken cloud, while absolute error is *lowest* there |
| H5 the best model by error is not the best by cost or interpretability | **Supported on cost.** Gradient boosting wins on both axes, so the accuracy-cost frontier contains only the tree models and the reference |

Three of five hypotheses failed, and the two that failed are the ones that would
have been invisible in a single-horizon, aggregate-metric study. That is the
strongest argument this project offers for the protocol it uses.
